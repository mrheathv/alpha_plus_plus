The Alpha++ Field Manual How a neon city grows, and what stops it

A city you zone, light, plumb and pay for. Every block is asking for something specific — land, water, power, a school, a road it can reach — and this manual is the list of what each one wants, in the order it will ask.

Founding a city

Three choices, and only one of them is cosmetic.

A new city starts in spring 1985 with $10,000 and a paused clock. One day passes per simulation tick, and a year is 360 days — about twelve minutes at normal speed.

Map size

32×32 is a town, 48×48 a city, 64×64 a metropolis. Size decides how far up the unlock ladder you can realistically climb: a 32×32 map tops out near 800 residents and earns everything but the stadium.

The land you start with

You do not own the map. The map you found is the region; the city begins on the central four parcels — a 16×16 square, a quarter of a small map and a sixteenth of a large one — and you buy the rest a parcel at a time.

A parcel is 8×8 tiles. The first costs $2,500, a quarter of your starting treasury, so buying land is something you do early rather than save up for; every parcel after it costs $2,500 more than the last. Buying out a whole 64×64 region runs to about $4.6M.

Two rules shape it. Land has to touch land you already own, so which parcel you take next is a decision about direction — toward the river, the coast, the edge a rail line needs to reach. And your rank caps how much you may own at all: see Ranks.

Buy land in the Land view. Pick it from the view picker and click a parcel. The cursor tells you whether it is for sale, out of reach, beyond your rank, or simply more than you can afford.

ShapeWhat it means for you

Water is a real constraint. Only roads cross it, at a surcharge of $150 a span ($300 for a highway). Pipes and power lines cross under a bridge, never through open water — so a bridge is the route for everything, not just the cars.

Your first city

The game will walk you through this in-game if you leave the guide switched on at founding. Here it is written down.

Everything below happens on the four parcels you start with. When you run out of room, buy another — see the land you start with.

  1. Lay a road

    Everything in a city hangs off its streets. Pick Road and drag a line across the map.

  2. Zone housing beside it

    Drag Residential along the road. A lot with nothing beside it never grows — access is the first gate, and it is checked before anything else.

  3. Give them somewhere to work

    Zone some Commercial and some Industrial too. People move in for work, so housing on its own empties out. A city with no jobs does not stall — it dies.

  4. Start the clock

    The city is paused when it is founded. Press Play, or Space, and it starts building what you zoned.

  5. Watch it grow

    Amber scaffolds are construction in progress. A storey takes days of real work, and it only rises while the city wants more of that kind of building.

  6. Bring water

    Past the second storey a block needs water. A water pump serves everything within four tiles with no pipe at all — put it next to the houses and it works.

  7. Switch the power on

    Higher still needs power. A generator works exactly the way the pump does, over the same radius.

  8. Find what's stuck

    The Problems view lights up every block that wants something, ranked. Hover one and the inspector names the fix in its first line.

  9. Protect it

    Fires and crime arrive with a bigger city — though nothing burns for the first 90 days. A fire station unlocks at 40 residents and stops a fire spreading.

  10. Visit City Hall

    ⌘B. Taxes, service funding, ordinances and loans all live there, and the budget is what decides how big the city can get.

Speed

Three settings, next to Play: Slow is four seconds a day, Normal two, Fast three quarters of a second. The city's slow systems — maintenance, the regional cycle — run over hundreds of days, so Slow is a genuine watch-it-breathe speed rather than a nuisance.

Tools that are not buildings

Pipes, power lines and transit routes are laid by clicking the map while their own view is up. Picking one of those tools raises its view for you; picking a zoning tool drops it again, so the two modes can never be confused. If a drag is not painting, check which view you are in.

How a block grows

The single most important mechanic in the game. A zoned lot climbs five levels, and each one has a gate.

Zoning a lot does not build anything. It marks the land; the city builds on it when — and only when — every gate below is open. Residential houses 4 people per level, so a full tower is 20 residents. Commercial and Industrial each provide 3 jobs per level.

The gates, in order

Before any level at all, a lot needs road or transit access, must not be on fire or damaged, and the city has to actually want more of that zone. Then:

  1. 1Access and demandNothing else required. A road beside it and a city that wants housing.
  2. 2Land value 0.30Plain road frontage alone gets close; a park or any service nearby clears it comfortably.
  3. 3Land value 0.50  +  waterThe first utility gate. A pump or tower in range, or a live pipe.
  4. 4Land value 0.65  +  powerWater is still required. A generator or plant in range, or a live line.
  5. 5Land value 0.80  +  a school in rangeThe top tier is something you go and build for, not something you stumble into.

Building takes time

An approved level is not an instant one. Construction costs 8 days for the first storey and 40 for the fifth — roughly four months to climb from bare ground to a finished tower, and a whole district takes about five months to settle. The amber wireframe on a lot is the volume that is coming; the bright deck climbing it is the work being done.

Work stops if the block is damaged, and losing road access cancels it outright. A site nobody can reach is not a site under construction.

And it runs backwards

Every gate is bidirectional. A lot that can no longer support the level it has loses one level at a time, slowly — about fifty days per level, so a district visibly rots rather than collapsing, and decline stops the moment you fix the cause. Defund your power, let a district choke on pollution, or let traffic seize up, and the towers come down a storey at a time.

Oversupply empties the worst blocks first. If you zone far more housing than the city wants, lots do not empty evenly — the marginal ones give way while desirable ones hold. Bad neighbourhoods are a real thing here, and they are the ones next to the factories.

Land value

The number behind three of the five growth gates. Everything good near a lot competes to be the best thing near it; the bad things stack.

Land value runs 0 to 1. Positives are a "best nearby" contest — a second police station beside the first adds nothing — with one exception. Penalties stack.

Parks are the tool for "this block is grim." They cost $120, occupy a single tile, and are available from the first day. Because they add rather than compete, a park is what carries an ordinary street over the 0.80 line for the top tier. They do not stack with each other, though — a wall of parks is worth one park, so paving the map in them buys nothing.

Pollution, and why where matters

Industry emits pollution in proportion to its density, over six tiles, and it accumulates. Crucially, not everyone minds equally: residents mind it fully, shops somewhat, factories barely. That asymmetry is the whole reason to plan a city rather than zone one homogeneous blob — put your industry together and downwind of your housing and both sides win. A planned layout measurably beats a mixed one with the identical zone mix.

Water and power

Two networks with identical rules, gating levels 3 and 4. Both have a capacity, not just a reach.

Three ways to be supplied

  • Near a source. Any pump, tower, generator or plant serves everything within 4 tiles of its footprint, with no pipe at all. This is the obvious move and it works.
  • Near a live conduit. A pipe or power line supplies everything within 3 tiles of it — provided that conduit traces back to a source through a connected run. Conduits cost $40 a tile.
  • Not at all. An orphaned run supplies nothing. In the Water and Power views, live conduits glow and dead ones are unlit wire.

So a source is for covering a neighbourhood and a trunk main is for reaching across a city. Run mains about seven tiles apart and their bands meet.

Capacity is the other half

Demand is the city's total built density across all growable zones. Each source supplies a fixed amount, scaled by its funding level:

Supply per building, at full funding
SourceCapacityCostUpkeep/day
Water pump60$250$8
Water tower200$700$20
Generator120$500$15

Over capacity, the whole network drops. Not the far end — all of it, city-wide, the way a blackout works. Growth stalls everywhere, the meter in the dashboard turns red, and one more plant fixes it. A city running on a single tower and a single plant reaches roughly half the population of one with enough utilities.

Leaving a grid overloaded also eats the lines carrying it, five times faster than normal wear. An overload is not a stable state you can ignore; it gets worse.

Maintenance

Roads, pipes and power lines wear out, and the public works budget is what holds them together. A quiet street wears slowly and full funding covers it comfortably; a saturated street wears five times faster and full funding does not. So what wears your city out is its own traffic — the arterials rot and the back streets do not — and a congested corridor has two honest answers: pay for it, or fix the congestion with a highway or a transit line.

A worn road carries less traffic, which makes it more congested. A conduit worn past three quarters stops conducting entirely, cutting the network exactly as a missing tile would. Neglect is dominated on both axes: a city that stops paying for public works ends up smaller and poorer.

Services and hazards

Coverage is a radius, not a connection. Funding scales it.

Every service building — police, fire, school, hospital — protects a 14-tile radius at full funding, scaling linearly with its budget: at half funding it reaches seven. That is a real dial, not a cliff. A 64×64 map wants roughly ten police and ten fire stations for full cover; a 40×40 wants about four of each.

Fire

Nothing burns for the first 90 days. A new city has neither built a fire station nor unlocked one, so the game does not raise a problem you cannot answer.

After that, a fire is a genuine disaster rather than an event: a struck block stays alight, and a block that is alight reaches for its neighbours each day. It only spreads to growable zones — your services are not kindling — and it cannot cross a street, which makes roads firebreaks without anyone designing them as such.

You have an answer right now: bulldozing a burning lot puts the fire out, at the cost of the building. That is a firebreak, and it is the trade the game intends you to make. Red streaks crossing the map are fire engines running from the station that covers the fire — if none appears, nothing covers it.

Measured: a row of eight adjacent industrial blocks with one alight loses 26 blocks over 25 runs with no fire station, and 1 with one.

Damage, and why it persists

A struck building records which service's absence let it happen, and it neither grows nor decays until that service covers it. Get a station in range and it repairs on the next day. Leave it uncovered and it rebuilds on its own eventually, at about 1% a day — so an unprotected district settles around half-broken: visibly blighted, permanently worse off, but alive.

Bulldozing and rebuilding clears damage too. That is the expensive escape hatch.

Traffic and transit

Everyone who lives somewhere tries to reach a job, and the journey is measured in minutes.

Each day the simulation routes every home to a job with room in it, weighing the trip in minutes. Congestion slows driving; a transit line that is genuinely faster wins the trip. Driving takes an exact tie — a journey that is no faster is not worth a walk and a wait.

That closes the loop the game runs on: roads fill, driving slows, trips move onto the lines, roads empty. Congestion also subtracts from land value and wears out the street.

Drawing a line

Stations carry the cost; the route is free. Pick a route tool, which raises its own view, then click the stations that are on the line. Click the last stop again to take it off. A line needs at least two working stops to carry anybody.

There is no track to draw and no vehicles to place — a bus runs on the roads you already have, a subway tunnels. Trams are the exception: they lay rails along the shortest road run between their stops, and that takes a quarter of the street's capacity.

What separates them

  • A bus shares the street. Park a line along a jammed arterial and it crawls, exactly when the city needs it most.
  • A tram has its own rails down the middle, so traffic barely touches it — but it takes a quarter of the lane. It is the only transit building in the game that costs the road something, and on a busy corridor it can genuinely make traffic worse. Measured on a mixed-use city, a tram carried twice a bus's riders and came out worse on congestion; on a city with long commutes, it beat the bus on both.
  • A subway is in a tunnel and does not care about traffic at all. Expensive, wide-reaching, and worth building before the city that justifies it.
  • Regional rail points off the map. Run a line so that a station sits within two tiles of the map edge and the whole line becomes regional — every stop on it is a way out, and residents can work outside the city.

Transfers

Two stations within 3 tiles of each other are an interchange, so you build one by siting stations near each other. A change costs 5 minutes plus a second wait — which is what makes not having to change worth something. Every leg counts a boarding, so a feeder line's ridership reflects the work it does.

Transit is worth what the commute is long. In a city where housing, shops and factories are interleaved every other lot, nearly every journey is three blocks and a bus's walk and wait swamp it. Separate your industry — which you want to do anyway, for the pollution — and transit starts earning its keep outright.

The bedroom-community trade

Outside jobs raise residential demand, because there is work to move here for, and lower commercial and industrial demand, because those residents are not filling a local job. A rail connection buys population and costs local business — and it is the only thing in the game that lets a city grow past what it can employ itself. It also ties you harder to the regional cycle: the region now reaches you twice.

The budget

Four levers, all in City Hall (⌘B), and all of them are tradeoffs rather than free wins.

Tax

Revenue is $1 per resident plus $2 per job, per day, multiplied by your rate — adjustable from 0 to 2.0 in quarter steps. A higher rate suppresses growth: measured over 1,500 days, rates of 0.0 / 1.0 / 2.0 produced 3,348 / 3,156 / 2,720 residents against a treasury of bankrupt / +$2.6M / +$14.0M. A real choice, not a slider to max out.

Funding

Each service has its own dial, 0 to 2.0. Funding buys reach for services, throughput for utilities, and repair for public works — it is never merely cosmetic. Over-funding genuinely over-delivers, and it will also bankrupt you: doubling every service's funding buys the most people of any strategy measured and sends the city broke.

Ordinances

Three city-wide policies, each costing $30 a day plus 5¢ per resident — so they scale with the city they cover.

Neighbourhood Watch
Halves the city-wide crime rate.
Fire Inspections
Halves the city-wide fire rate.
Business Tax Break
Nudges commercial demand up.

What the money is for

Beyond upkeep, the treasury's real job is land. Every parcel costs more than the last, so expansion is a sink that grows with you rather than a purchase you finish — and the three rank rewards, at $3,000, $8,000 and $25,000, are the only other things worth saving for.

Bonds

$5,000 at a time, up to a cap of $15,000 plus $10 per resident, at 0.25% of the balance per day. Borrowing early is close to pointless — the cap is at its floor when nobody lives there — and borrowing once the city exists costs a real but modest slice of revenue. The whole cost of a bond is the interest; the debt does not feed back into the tax base servicing it.

What you are paying for

Beyond building upkeep, a city pays 75¢ per day per resident and per job in civic costs. That is deliberately charged on the same axis tax is collected on, so it scales with the city instead of going flat the moment you stop building. It is also kept under $1, so every resident and job stays worth having.

The region outside

Your city is not a closed system. The region booms and busts on its own clock.

Residential, commercial and industrial demand each rise and fall on cycles of roughly ten to fourteen months, independently of anything you do. It is a clock, not a dice roll, so you can learn it and plan against it — and the three sectors are decoupled, which is the part that makes it a decision. Your city can be short of jobs while housing is oversupplied, and the answer is to re-zone, using the demand meter you already have.

The swing is smaller than your own tax lever, on purpose: the region can make a bad tax rate hurt, but it cannot overrule a good one. In a slump, the marginal lots — the ones already at the bottom — cross into abandonment while the desirable ones hold.

Freight: ports and airports

Late-game, and the only things in the game that raise a whole sector's demand by themselves. A seaport ($1,800, 3×3, must touch water) raises industrial demand; an airport ($2,600, 3×3, needs nine tiles of dry ground) raises commercial.

Each gives diminishing returns — the first port is the one that connects the city at all, and every one after only widens a connection that already exists — and each is among the heaviest things in the game to run. An airport costs $75 a day, more than a hospital — beaten only by the Arcology, which a Metropolis earns.

Zone colours

Hue says which zone, and it shifts with density rather than merely brightening:

  • Residential — violet at low density, orchid in the middle, pale lavender for towers.
  • Commercial — pink retail, hot rose offices, amber entertainment.
  • Industrial — orange manufacturing, red-orange heavy industry, brick red for the worst polluters.
  • Parks are the one green in the game, because a park is the thing that is not built.

The silhouettes differ too, not just the colour: industry is wide and low with sawtooth roofs and chimneys, housing is stepped with punched window grids and balconies, commerce has continuous glazing bands and lit crowns. You can tell the three apart with the colour taken away.

Landmarks

About one top-tier lot in twelve is drawn as a landmark instead of an ordinary building — a slender crowned spire for commerce, a point block on a podium for housing, a works with one enormous stack for industry. It is purely visual, with no effect on the simulation, and it is what stops a built-out downtown reading as a bar chart. Which lots get one is fixed, so a landmark stays a landmark across saves and launches.

Zoomed in

The inspector

Hover any block. The first line names a fix, not a mechanism — "Needs water", then "put a tower or a pump within a few tiles" — and everything below it is supporting evidence: density, residents, coverage, and how long the commute is and by what mode. If a lot says nothing is wrong, nothing is: it is waiting on demand.

Ranks

Five of them, earned in order. A rank is the only thing that decides how much land you may own, and it is what three buildings are gated behind.

A rank is not a population threshold with a nicer name. Each one asks for a city that is working, not merely large — served, schooled, solvent, and not choking on its own traffic — and the requirements are checked together, so you cannot trade one away against another.

Read the ladder as a checklist for what to fix. Village asks for water, Town adds power and schools, City adds pollution and a balanced budget, Metropolis adds traffic and repair. That is the same order the systems in this manual bite in — the ranks are the game telling you which one to look at next.

Common mistakes

Measured on real cities, over 1,500 days each. Best to worst strategy is a 33× spread in population, so these matter.

Zoning only housing
The worst thing you can do. With no jobs, demand pins at the floor and every lot empties regardless of how nice it is: 100 residents and bankrupt. Oversupply is not a stall here, it is a collapse.
Building services before utilities
Services unlock the growth that utilities have to support. A city with services and no water or power measured 1,740 residents and bankrupt, against 3,320 and solvent once the utilities arrived.
One tower and one plant
Capacity, not connection, is what limits a utility. A 64×64 city on a single tower and plant reaches 1,976 people against 3,580 with enough of them, and spends every day overloaded.
Mixing industry into housing
Pollution accumulates and residents mind it most. Zoning industry apart is the single best measured layout decision: 3,760 residents against 3,320 for the same zone mix, and more money too.
Maxing every funding dial
It does buy the most people of any strategy — and it bankrupts you. Normal spacing plus the utilities to support it beats dense, over-funded services on both axes.
Ignoring public works
A city that stops paying for maintenance loses 40% of its built density and ends up with less money than one that pays. It is dominated on both axes, which is what makes it a lever rather than a tax.
Laying a tram down your busiest street
It takes a quarter of that street's capacity. On a short-commute city that cancels out everything it carries. Trams are for long corridors.
Panicking at a fire
Bulldoze the burning block. You lose one building and stop a district burning.

If you only remember one thing: open the Problems view, find the loudest block, hover it, and do what the first line says. The whole simulation is designed so that every warning it raises has an answer you can act on immediately.
